Sampling Lab

Interactive tools for sampling variability, sampling distributions, the Central Limit Theorem, and the sampling distribution of a sample proportion. Every tool uses the same three colors: population, one sample, and the sampling distribution (many samples). Keep those three apart and most of this unit falls into place.

Tool 1

Sampling variability

Draw 5 of 30 men at random and watch x̄ change from sample to sample. Then list every possible sample.

 Tool 2

Sampling distributions & CLT

Build the sampling distribution of x̄ one sample at a time, from normal and very non-normal populations.

 Tool 3

Probabilities with z

Standardize x̄ or p̂, find probabilities, and check the answer by simulating real samples.

 Tool 4

Sample proportions

Draw beads from an urn and see when p̂ is (and isn’t) approximately normal.

Each link below opens a tool already set up for that step of the lecture. Every setup is stored in the URL, so “Copy link” on any page gives you a link that reproduces it.

Lecture 1: A statistic varies from sample to sample

  1. Draw samples of 5 from 30 men. Draw one at a time on Slow. Each x̄ misses μ by a different amount. That variability is the subject of the unit.
  2. Draw 100 samples (press H). Compare the two rows: the means cluster much more tightly than the individual heights.
  3. Show all 142,506 possible samples. This is the exact sampling distribution, with no simulation involved. Its mean is exactly μ.

Lecture 2: The sampling distribution of x̄

  1. n = 1. With samples of one, the sampling distribution is just the population.
  2. n = 4, then n = 16, then n = 64. The x-axis stays fixed, so the narrowing is visible. Each time n is quadrupled, the SD is halved: σ/√n.
  3. Compare 4, 16, 64 side by side.
  4. Normal population ⇒ x̄ is normal even for n = 2.
  5. Contrast: the sample maximum. Its average misses the population max, so “μx̄ = μ” is a special property of the mean, not something every statistic has.

Lecture 3: The Central Limit Theorem

  1. One die, two dice, five dice. The distribution goes from flat to a triangle to a bell.
  2. Skewed waiting times: n = 2, 5, 30.
  3. Bimodal geyser eruptions. Two humps still turn into one bell.
  4. The data do not become normal. A sample of 500 waiting times is still skewed, because it looks like the population. Only the distribution of x̄ becomes normal.
  5. Income, n = 30 and scratch-off tickets, n = 30. “n ≥ 30” is a rule of thumb. The more skewed the population, the larger n has to be.

Lecture 4: Standardizing x̄

  1. P(one man > 71 in) vs. P(average of 9 men > 71 in). Use the same cut-off for both. The probability is about 1 in 4 for one man and about 1 in 40 for the average of 9.
  2. Skewed population, small n. The z answer and the simulation disagree. Raise n and they converge.
  3. Any textbook problem: enter μ, σ, n, and the cut-offs.

Lecture 5: The sampling distribution of p̂

  1. Coin flips, n = 25. p̂ is the mean of 0/1 data. That’s why σp̂ = √(p(1−p)/n) is just σ/√n with σ = √(p(1−p)).
  2. Same shrinking story: n = 10, 50, 200.
  3. p = 0.02, n = 50, then n = 1,000. np < 10 gives a skewed, gappy distribution. With enough expected successes it becomes normal.
  4. Poll question: if 52% of voters support a measure, how often does a poll of 400 show a minority?

For recording

  • Presenter mode (F): larger text and plots, and the seed and shortcut boxes are hidden.
  • Seeds: the same seed and the same clicks give the same samples, so a flubbed take can be redone identically.
  • Keyboard: Space draws one sample, T draws 10, K draws 1,000, P plays/pauses, R resets, O toggles the normal curve, Z zooms, C compares.
  • Animation speed: use Slow for the first sample or two, then switch to Fast or draw 1,000 at once.

All data are simulated. Populations are fixed lists of 10,000 values, so μ and σ are exact properties of what’s on screen.
